# Preface

<center><img src="https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/a/a5/Marx_Engels_oval.svg" alt="马克思与恩格斯" width="25%"></center>

Nearly two centuries ago, Karl Marx, with his profound insight, delivered an incisive critique of the capitalist system that represented the pinnacle of humanity's old civilization, with alienation and reification at the core of his theory. Marx pointed out that the laborer's labor is alienated, with their creativity, freedom, and life transformed into an external force dominating themselves, while individual freedom, self-realization, and even species-being are cruelly reified; the bourgeoisie, in pursuing profit and power, likewise falls into the alienation of its own mode of existence—they maintain the system through violence, yet are devoured in turn by bloody, anti-human acts; the logic of capital and power reifies the ruling class itself, stripping it of control over its own actions and social relations. As a result, the social relations between people are thoroughly alienated and reified, becoming an objective force independent of humanity, dominating and distorting every individual.

Even more strikingly, on this basis Marx and Engels jointly founded the theory of scientific communism, revealing the historical inevitability that human society will ultimately move toward an era of public ownership that abolishes classes and exploitation. In this theory, distribution according to need means not only the rational allocation of consumer goods according to individuals' actual needs, but is also reflected more profoundly in the comprehensive public provision of social resources such as education, healthcare, and public services. It presupposes public ownership of the means of production and the thorough elimination of class exploitation, achieving a fundamental reconstruction of social relations, thereby laying a solid institutional foundation for the free and comprehensive development of human beings.

This theory is a breathtakingly bold vision in the history of human thought, providing both a starting point for critique and a coordinate of ideals for all subsequent theories of social transformation.

Marx believed that the society he envisioned was the truly human society, and that the various social forms appearing in our history to date are the prehistory of the truly human society. In other words, we have not yet reached the truly human society—because the relations between people follow, in fact, the ethical principles of the animal kingdom. The mutual confrontation between people brought freedom from the natural world, but at a tremendous cost. The prehistory of human society will come to an end with the social form of capitalism! Marx said that in a truly human society, the free development of each individual is the condition for the free development of all! As long as one person is not free, there can be no human freedom! [^1]

Yet Feuerbach's "species-being" (Gattungswesen), criticized by Marx from the perspective of historical materialism as "abstract," is in fact a profoundly humanist insight: in Feuerbach's vision, the reason a human is a "species-being" lies in the universality of the human horizon—the entire natural world and human society can both become objects of human cognition and contemplation. More crucially, the three forces of reason, will, and love constitute the intrinsic essence of the human being. They are by no means the private wealth of the individual, but a universal essence shared by all humanity. It is precisely in the fusion of reason, will, and love that the individual is able to confirm their existence as a member of "humankind." However, this "love" as Feuerbach understood it still remains essentially at the level of sensory intuition and passive reception, and still lacks the practical dimension of active engagement with the external world—this limitation not only laid the groundwork for Marx's subsequent critical "sublation," but also provides a key point of support for us to re-examine and construct the complete structure of "love."

Because Marx overlooked the profundity of Feuerbach's theory of "species-being," he failed to provide answers convincing enough to command general assent in later generations on two fundamental questions: Why did humanity develop the highly unequal social form of capitalism? And what, ultimately, is the terminal force in humanity that drives the continuous expansion of alienation and reification?

Moreover, although Marx and Engels both explored "whether humanity can transition to a better social form by peaceful means," they never proposed a reliable global solution.

The nearly two hundred years of social practice of Marxist thought, accompanied by the rapid development of technology—especially the recent explosive development of large language models representing human intelligence—have provided us with extraordinarily rich inspiration and intellectual supplementation, enabling us, on the basis of inheriting the far-sighted insights of Marx, Feuerbach, and other predecessors, to continue digging deeper into the essence of human civilizational governance and to propose, in a transcendent manner, a new consensus mechanism for the governance of the next human civilization: Proof of Love2 (PoL2)!
