\begin{abstract}
Typed decision models return a probability over declared options, not text, so screening all messages is cheap.
We ask whether they can serve as safeguards for PoL governance under Proof of Love2 (\pol{}), an open text prescribing a ``safety valve'' against hate language, AI-only adjudication of welfare disputes and per-person assessment.
We derive seven testable requirements from its clauses and assess them against \nPapers{} studies and \nGrey{} grey sources on the first such model, \jev{}, and open counterparts in its first 17 days; author-directed AI agents read in full, appraised and coded the \nCoded{} that bear on them (blind second coding: $\kappa = \nKappa{}$).
The evidence supports typed models as detectors under added conditions: they rank risky content well at a fraction of LLM evaluators' cost, and in one preregistered study on simulated data accepting low-risk verdicts only when an independently built rule agreed lost no accuracy, though disguised attacks passed.
It does not support them as judges: thresholds do not transfer, decisions can follow option names and be steered by an inserted opinion, ``I don't know'' or ``none'' options are chosen inconsistently, and peer evaluators repeated almost all of a typed model's confident errors.
Typed models were not generally worse than LLM evaluators, but most failures lack a comparison; an update to 8 October reversed no answer.
Five tensions in the text follow, set against EU law: emotion recognition, binary verdicts, closed-model transparency, AI-only adjudication and per-person scoring.
Our verdict for \pol{}, at low or very low certainty: on current evidence \jev{} is not a suitable tool for any function that decides and can serve the safety valve only as a recorded, three-valued, locally calibrated detector whose consequential decisions end with a person, as we propose; even that extrapolates from English benchmarks, as no study measures \pol{}'s construct in Chinese.
\end{abstract}
