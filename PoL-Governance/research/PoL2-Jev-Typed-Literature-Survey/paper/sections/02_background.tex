\subsection{Typed decision models}
\label{sec:tdm}

\paragraph{Interface.}
A typed decision model receives a \emph{state} (text or structured data) and one or more questions, each with an option set declared by the caller.
For each question it returns a probability distribution over exactly those options and generates no free text~\cite{arx23886,arx26758,arx32160}.
\jev{} offers three question types~\cite{typesafe_intro,arx32160}:
\emph{Choice} selects among listed options (up to 255) and returns probabilities and a confidence value;
\emph{Score} rates the state against ordered rubric levels;
\emph{Noul} asks whether a statement is true and returns the probability of ``yes''.
Many questions about one state can be answered in a single call~\cite{arx29429,arx28919}.
In a census of 2{,}170 public projects using \jev{} by 22~September, Choice appeared in 81.0\% of projects, Noul in 72.2\% and Score in 45.4\%~\cite{arx30216}.

\paragraph{Readout and training.}
The hosted model is closed.
TypeSafe describes a ``parallel sampler'' and training by \emph{reinforcement learning for calibrated decisions} (RLCD), and publishes the calibration property this training targets (among outcomes given probability $p$, a fraction close to $p$ should occur), but not the architecture, the reward, the data or the weights~\cite{almeida2026introducing,typesafe_primer,arx24574,arx28919}.
Open-weight models with the same interface appeared within days and make the mechanism inspectable.
They fall into three readout families~\cite{arx32160}:
(i) decoders that score the hidden state at a designated answer position against the embeddings of the option labels and normalise over the declared options only (this-that-model-1.0, JevLite, SemIf, Visual Jev)~\cite{arx23886,arx23959,arx22753,arx25845};
(ii) encoders that read a marker token placed at each option (Laya, a ModernBERT model)~\cite{arx26758}; and
(iii) encoders that pool the option name and its definition (Open-Jev, a DeBERTa model)~\cite{arx26758}.
Readouts of the same kind can also be taken from frozen instruction-tuned LLMs without further training~\cite{arx00831,arx02076}.
Two open models train with cross-entropy plus a Brier-score term~\cite{arx23886,arx23959}, and one open implementation of RLCD uses a REINFORCE loop rewarding the outcome minus the probability placed on the chosen option~\cite{arx24574}.

\paragraph{What ``type-safe'' guarantees.}
In open models the scores of options outside the declared set are masked before normalisation, so every answer is a valid member of the option set by construction; for hosted \jev{} this is the documented behaviour~\cite{arx26758,typesafe_intro}.
This guarantees \emph{form}, not \emph{correctness}~\cite{arx26758}; calibration is a separate empirical property (\cref{sec:g4}).
Hosted \jev{} is not deterministic: identical repeated requests change a small share of labels, and probabilities are reported on a two-decimal grid~\cite{arx32160,arx01006}.
The vendor's documentation lists nine known failure modes for \texttt{jev-1.13}, including literal reading, arithmetic, dates, indirection, large irrelevant state, adversarial content and contradictory instructions; a version of the page archived on 20~September also gave an example in which a statement and its complement received probabilities 0.72 and 0.47~\cite{typesafe_jaggedness}.

\paragraph{Cost and latency.}
The vendor lists \$0.042 per million input tokens with free output~\cite{typesafe_models}, and its launch post cites 70--500\,ms per call~\cite{almeida2026introducing}.
Independent client-side medians range from about 140 to 500\,ms depending on workload and request size~\cite{arx26550,arx27535,arx28919,arx22753,arx23136,arx27331,arx00346}.
Relative to generative evaluators, reported cost reductions range from a few-fold to two orders of magnitude depending on the comparator and the pricing assumed: for alignment-failure detection, 63$\times$ pooled at list prices but about 12$\times$ under conservative repricing~\cite{arx29429}; for population simulation, 8$\times$ against a mid-tier model and 312--455$\times$ against a flagship model~\cite{arx27535}.
The advantage is not universal: in one edge-orchestration study a hosted low-cost generative model was billed less than \jev{}~\cite{arx23136}, and inside a multi-step agent loop the hosted model took 1.4--2.1 times as long end to end, including API calls, as a local open scorer~\cite{arx00437}.

\subsection{Related work}
\label{sec:related}

The questions raised by typed safeguards are new instances of older problems, and we use the earlier literature to judge which findings are new.

\paragraph{Automated content moderation.}
Platform moderation has long combined automated classifiers with human review, and its critics have shown that automation hides contestable policy choices inside technical systems, shifts error costs onto users and resists accountability~\cite{gorwa2020algorithmic,gillespie2018custodians}.
Safety classifiers that read token probabilities, such as Llama Guard~\cite{inan2023llama}, moderation models~\cite{markov2023holistic} and constitutional classifiers~\cite{sharma2025constitutional}, and programmable guardrails~\cite{rebedea2023nemo} are the closest technical precursors of a typed safety valve; prompt injection~\cite{perez2022ignore,greshake2023not,zhan2024injecagent} and adaptive adversarial attacks~\cite{zou2023universal,toyer2024tensor} are the corresponding threats.

\paragraph{Hate speech, annotator disagreement and Chinese resources.}
Automated hate-speech detection shows racial and dialect bias~\cite{sap2019risk,davidson2019racial}, fails targeted functional tests~\cite{rottger2021hatecheck}, and depends on annotators' beliefs and identities~\cite{sap2022annotators}.
Where annotators disagree, aggregating labels by majority vote discards information that a safeguard needs~\cite{davani2022dealing,plank2022problem,pavlick2019inherent}.
Chinese offensive-language and toxicity benchmarks exist~\cite{deng2022cold,lu2023toxicn,jiang2022swsr}, but none labels the constructs of \pol{}.

\paragraph{Emotion recognition.}
Inferring emotional states from observable signals rests on weak scientific foundations~\cite{barrett2019emotional}, and emotion recognition by AI systems raises distinct ethical risks of manipulation and of misreading people~\cite{stark2021ethics}; the EU AI Act prohibits emotion recognition from biometric data in workplaces and education, a definition that does not reach inference from text alone~\cite{euaiact2024}.

\paragraph{Calibration, abstention and deferral.}
Calibration of probabilistic predictions has a long literature, from Brier's score~\cite{brier1950verification} and Platt scaling~\cite{platt1999probabilistic} to the miscalibration of modern networks~\cite{guo2017calibration,naeini2015obtaining} and language models~\cite{jiang2021can,kadavath2022language,tian2023just,xiong2024can}; calibration degrades under distribution shift~\cite{ovadia2019trust} and can hold on average while failing for subgroups~\cite{hebertjohnson2018multicalibration}.
Abstention goes back to Chow's reject option~\cite{chow1970optimum} and selective classification~\cite{geifman2017selective,kamath2020selective,wen2025know}; learning to defer formalises when a model should hand a case to a human expert~\cite{madras2018predict,mozannar2020consistent}, and conformal risk control gives distribution-free error guarantees under exchangeability~\cite{bates2021distribution,angelopoulos2024conformal}.

\paragraph{Sensitivity and evaluator reliability.}
Language models are sensitive to option order, label names and prompt format~\cite{zhao2021calibrate,lu2022fantastically,zheng2024large,pezeshkpour2024large,sclar2024quantifying,wei2023larger}, which is one reason holistic evaluation reports many scenarios and metrics rather than one score~\cite{liang2023holistic}.
LLM evaluators show position bias~\cite{wang2024fair}, favour their own outputs~\cite{panickssery2024llm} and make correlated errors~\cite{goel2025great}; panels of diverse evaluators~\cite{verga2024replacing} and cascades that escalate uncertain items to a stronger model~\cite{zheng2023judging,chen2023frugalgpt,jung2025trust} are the established remedies.
The typed setting removes generation but, as \cref{sec:evidence} shows, not these problems.

\paragraph{Human oversight and due process.}
Human oversight of automated decisions often fails: people over-rely on confident systems~\cite{parasuraman2010complacency}, oversight policies assume abilities reviewers lack~\cite{green2022flaws,green2019principles}, human--AI teams do not reliably beat the better member~\cite{bansal2021whole}, and responsibility can be shifted onto the nearest human operator~\cite{elish2019moral}.
Automated adjudication also threatens notice, reasons and the opportunity to contest~\cite{citron2008technological,wachter2018counterfactual,doshivelez2017towards}, which data-protection, platform and AI law now partly codify~\cite{gdpr2016,dsa2022,euaiact2024,santaclara2021}, alongside risk-management frameworks~\cite{nist2023airmf}.
Documentation practices for models and datasets~\cite{mitchell2019model,gebru2021datasheets} inform what a decision record should contain.

\paragraph{Algorithmic governance, DAOs and social scoring.}
Decentralised autonomous organisations encode governance in software~\cite{buterin2014ethereum,hassan2021decentralized,santana2022blockchain}, continuing older work on governing common resources~\cite{ostrom1990governing}, and raise the concern that rule by algorithm narrows human participation~\cite{danaher2016threat}.
Value specifications for AI, such as constitutions used in training~\cite{bai2022constitutional}, are the nearest analogue to \pol{}'s Ethical Alignment Protocol.
Systems that score individuals' behaviour for public purposes, such as China's social credit system, show how such scores become infrastructure for control~\cite{creemers2018social,liang2018constructing}.
